# The Ambassador Program: a creative platform, and the research behind it

Second pass, 2026-09-27. The first pass audited the onboarding screen by screen. This one starts where a strategy, copy and design team would: with the person and the truth, then one idea, then every line and moment made to carry it. What it rests on: The Labs verbatim (its site, brand kit, the OLOS repo and its registration funnel, press, LinkedIn and Luma); Red Antler's method in its founders' words and its critics' words; this repo's git history; and three decisions from the client: the hero copy and its product-launch vibe stay; the pin becomes a Labs-wide signifier for the people who keep The Labs running (the tier's name is still open); the button is handed over in person by a coordinator at the event where someone signs up.

Sections 1 to 3 are the platform. Section 4 is what was built. The appendices hold the research, the corrections to the first pass, and the sources.

## 1. Strategy

**The person.** Someone The Labs changed. Most came in during a career shock (the pilot, run by DC Public Library and Levy: fifty former federal workers chosen from two hundred applicants), found people, built something, and left more capable. The alums of that pilot founded The Labs. The Urban Libraries Council's survey of that cohort: 89% more confident, 73% made five or more useful connections. The moment they became an ambassador in their heart came before any program: the first time they thought "I know exactly who needs this." A former colleague. A neighbour. Someone standing where they stood a year ago.

**The person we're writing to.** Someone who came through The Labs: a workshop regular, a Build Cycle alum, maybe one of the founding fifty. Mid-career, more often than not, and usually here because work changed under their feet. They found people, built something real with a team, and left more capable than they arrived. They know the coordinator by first name. They've already told one or two people about The Labs without being asked. What they want, underneath: to matter to people in a way no job title gives them; to be the person others come to for a way through; to be early to something good and to have had a hand in it; to be useful in their own neighborhood, in public, with their name on it; to leave something that outlasts them. What they're afraid of: sounding like a salesperson, overselling a young organization, not knowing the answer to a hard question, being "that person" at a party, time. Their ideal self: the friend who went first. A founder without the ego, the one who was in the room when there was no building, who brought the next ten people, and who can say so for the rest of their life. The scout leader, the volunteer who opened the Y on Saturday mornings: ordinary people who made an institution out of a practice, one town at a time.

**Where it's going.** The Labs isn't a place. In the founders' words it is "a corpus a place and its people adopt and help grow and improve": place-based programming and community building that makes room for networks to form, communities to be built, and practices and methods to be run and improved. Public copy says "something a region picks up, makes its own, and makes better", to stay at the brand's reading level. The unit is the metro region, multi-jurisdictional and centered on a socio-economic hub (Greater Washington, the national capital region, the DMV), with neighborhoods second. The hope, always said as a hope: that it becomes a normal part of American life the way scouting and the YMCA did, something every region has, that neighbors run, that outlasts the people who started it. The organisation is a year old (founded January 2026 by the alums of the fall 2025 pilot run by DC Public Library and Levy, a strategic design firm) and volunteer-run, so "Recruiting our founding ambassadors" on the hero is the same idea: the people who carry it now are the ones who make the hope true or not. On work, with Arendt named here and nowhere public: The Labs does want people more competitive in the current and coming job market, but it sees most jobs that are mostly labor being automated and jobs shifting toward work (making what lasts) and action (doing together in public). Nobody knows what will be hired for next, so the curriculum is modular and the organisation is built to shift as the technology and society shift, and, where it can, to help lead those shifts. Across every region, The Labs aims to become a practice-based research network doing real, distributed, open R&D on that question, one Build Cycle at a time.

**The truth.** Ambassadors don't need persuading that The Labs is worth sharing. What they need is a nudge to think of a specific person, and then the words and the nerve: how to bring it up without sounding like a pitch, what to say when someone asks "is this a bootcamp?", what to do with a room. The guide already is the words and the nerve (your story, the conversation, the room, practice). The program page's job comes one step earlier: get the reader to picture someone. Suzie Zhang, from the pilot, said the thing itself to Technical.ly: "Burnout is real, and it's great that you have so many more people in the same boat."

**The idea: Think of someone who's figuring out what's next.** A prompt, not a claim about the reader. It is offered on every surface in a different register, and answered, when the person is ready, with a name on the "You're in" screen. It describes the person The Labs is for (the pilot's own words: "navigating career change") rather than promising what The Labs will do for them. It reframes the category the way Red Antler reframes categories (Hinge is "designed to be deleted"; Keeps makes prevention "a point of pride"): not a referral program with a code, but a way to act on a person you can picture.

**The tension we keep on purpose.** A product launch for something nobody's selling. The page is staged like a hardware launch (the cinematic pin, the chapter covers, "Recruiting our founding ambassadors"), and the twist is that the product can't be bought and the thing being launched is you. Heyward's "embrace tension" principle: name it, and the page stops feeling borrowed and starts feeling like a point of view.

**Who we're not** (Heyward: "figure out not just who you are, but who you're not"). Not a sales job: nothing's for sale. Not a recruiter: people join themselves. Not a script: your story, your words. Not a rank: the pin marks a contribution, not a title.

**Personality.** The friend who went first. Dry, specific, generous. Speaks in names and dates, never adjectives. The best existing lines already have it: "That's it. That's the step." "It can't be bought, only passed on." "Earned, not given." The Labs' own site has it too: "Every lab started as a list of names. Enough names, and we come."

**Tone.** Inviting before anything else. The idea is offered, not asserted: "think of someone", never "you already know who." No absolutes about the reader, no "never", no promises about what The Labs will do for anyone. Commands only where the reader has asked to be told what to do, which is the guide.

## 2. Verbal system

**Hero.** Unchanged: "Ambassador Program" as the wordmark, "A volunteer corps for people who can't keep a good thing to themselves", "Recruiting our founding ambassadors".

**The argument, on the program page and in the guide.** Under "It starts with you.", before the three cards, and again in The Labs step as "Why it needs you": four headers in the ambassador's voice about who they get to be, which read as one paragraph on their own. *You found your way through. Be the one who says "come with me." Be how it reaches the next neighborhood. Be early to something built to last. Bring someone.* The bodies keep The Labs humble: the reader did the getting and The Labs just made room; it is a year old and volunteer-run; the vision is stated as the only way it could happen, not a plan; nobody has the future of work figured out. The Labs step also gains three questions ambassadors get: whether it's just a DC thing, what it actually teaches (practitioners and Upskillers teaching, a modular curriculum that shifts with the tech, a Build Cycle fast and on a team or a self-paced project), and what "the future of work" means (not only jobs; work and action).

**The cut.** Read as a Red Antler site designer and copywriter, the program page carried 1,460 words; it now carries about 760, most of them headers and labels. Every lede is one line, every body one sentence, each fact said once, placeholders moved out of the copy. The argument's four headers keep their one-liners; the long bodies survive only in this report as the reasoning behind them.

**The phone pass.** On a 390px phone the page was 16,243px tall (about 19 screens, 7 of them the three chapter covers), the sticky header took up to 120px of every screen, the hero's buttons wrapped below the fold, text blocks stayed half-transparent while being read (the reveal animation ran over each block's own height), the covers were served at 900w for a frame that renders about 1,350px wide in portrait, the 3D pin and a 4MB video loaded on any phone wider than 480px, and the "Who's first?" input was unstyled. Now: covers at 150vh with portrait crops (`npm run covers`), the header 74px once scrolled with the link row easing in, the primary CTA above the fold with "See why" as a text link, reveals tied to the viewport, a 188KB MP4 and 278KB WebM for phones, the 3D pin gated to hover-and-pointer devices from 900px with its loop paused off screen, safe-area gutters, 16px inputs, 44px hit areas, no filter animations on phones, the agreement scrolling with the page, and the quiz waiting on a Next button after a pass so the explanations can be read. The page is now 13,974px tall on a phone.

**Inside the three chapters.** The three full-screen pin close-ups are the page's chapter titles, and each chapter's opener flows from its cover (that structure was deliberate, commit deea568). The taglines stay: **Spark curiosity. Build community. Inspire confidence.** They are the framework; the idea lives inside it, as the first thing each chapter says and the last thing the page says.

| Chapter | Cover | How the idea enters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Spark curiosity. | "It starts with you." / "Someone got you curious about The Labs. Now think of someone who's figuring out what's next. Give a little time. It pays you back three ways." Then "Worth being curious about." shows what you'd be bringing them into. |
| 2 | Build community. | "Curiosity goes both ways." (what you'll do) · "One conversation. Then a network." · "What we're building, together." with the founding-ten roster under it: community, one name at a time. |
| 3 | Inspire confidence. | "The button says you're in. The pin says you passed it on." (the four-verb ladder) · the questions · "What it takes." · the close: "Come be an ambassador." / "Think of someone who's figuring out what's next. Ten minutes to get in. Everything after, we'll figure out together." |

Where the prompt appears, in order: the meta description; the first chapter's lede; the page's close; the pass ("Who's first?" / "Think of someone who's figuring out what's next. Type their first name, and your phone sends the invite."); the guide's finish ("You're ready. Who's first?").

**The ladder, four verbs, on every surface.** Raise your hand. Get in. Get ready. Pass it on. "Get in" is ten minutes on your phone and ends with the coordinator handing you the button. "Get ready" is the guide, about half an hour, and ends on "You're ready. You know who." "Pass it on" is [N] new Upskillers, one talk, one new ambassador, and ends with the pin. The guide's old finish line ("finish five steps to earn the pin") predated the mockup and was never reconciled with it; now there is one ladder.

**The manifesto**, which opens orientation's short read (the read goes on to cover every fact the coordinator's ten questions ask about):

1. Most people find The Labs because someone they know said "come with me."
2. The Labs is free. Workshops, Build Cycles, all of it. Run by volunteers. It grew out of a fall 2025 pilot run by DC Public Library and Levy, a local strategic design firm. The pilot's alums founded The Labs.
3. People pick the problems. Small teams build real things. Everyone teaches what they learn.
4. Your job is three moves. Ask what they're working toward. Share thirty seconds of your story. Invite them to one dated next step.
5. You don't sell, sign anyone up, or promise a job. People Join The Labs themselves at theupskillinglabs.org.
6. If anyone asks what we are: "a fiscally sponsored project of Superbloom Design, a 501(c)(3). Our own status is pending."

**Orientation, in the order that gives before it takes.** The Labs in three minutes (the film, or the read) → the quiz → the agreement → your name → You're in. The quiz is the ambassador coordinator's ten-question certification (tagline, why The Labs exist, Build Cycles, pods, no technical background, the two skill sets, the origin, open source, Google sign-in), with their hints and explanations and their pass mark of eight. Its lede keeps their framing: "It's not about being perfect, it's about being ready to tell the story."

**The pass.** "You're in." The button, large on ink. "Maya Chen · Ambassador." "Show this to your coordinator. They'll hand you your button." Then the signature question: **Who's first?** "Think of someone who's figuring out what's next. Type their first name, and your phone sends the invite." One name; the same dated message the Conversation step sends; the name goes to the front of that step's list. Then a text link to say hi (the coordinator message now reads like a person wrote it, with the record-keeping in a trailing parenthesis), and "Get ready: open the guide."

**The voice, as guardrails.** OLOS's design system, §11, is the only written voice spec The Labs has: "second person, plain language, ~14-year-old reading level"; "Confident but not bossy"; "Encouraging, not effusive. 'Submitted.' not 'Awesome! 🎉'". The brand kit: "Warm, Clear, Grounded. Build · Practice · Momentum · Together"; avoid "Disrupt · Leverage · Ecosystem · Innovation"; sentence case. The live site's register: short declaratives and fragments, lists of three, "you" everywhere, "show up", "something real", "there's a place for you here", doors ("one door for everyone", "Same door"). House style here stays: no em dashes, no exclamation points, one idea per line. The copy linter now also bans "on a mission to", "passionate" and "thoughtfully", the three bland tells the first draft was closest to.

**Casing, from the live site.** Upskiller (always capitalised), pod (lowercase), Build Cycle, "Join The Labs" in buttons. The Labs' own site lowercases "the" mid-sentence; this site never does: always "The Upskilling Labs" or "The Labs", never a bare "Labs", never a plural verb, and the copy linter now enforces it. Fixed across the site. "Poderator" now carries a two-word gloss the first time an ambassador meets it.

**The tier name** (the client's open question; site copy stays name-free until it's decided). Constraints: volunteers, no statutory membership; an umbrella over mentors, Poderators, organisers and ambassadors; fits The Labs' vocabulary; sentence-case friendly; not member, fellow, partner or staff.

| Candidate | For | Against |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Crew** (recommended) | What you call the people who run something together without owning it. One syllable. Sits naturally beside "pods". "Join the crew" implies work, not membership. Reads well on a backstamp. | Casual; a film or boat word if you squint. |
| **Corps** | Civic-service register in DC (Peace Corps, AmeriCorps). Already the ambassador page's word. Explicitly volunteer. | Militaristic to some ears; pronunciation; on this site it currently means ambassadors specifically. |
| Friends of The Labs | The library world's model for volunteer supporters, and The Labs is library-born. | "Friends" groups read as donors and are often separate 501(c)(3)s. |
| Contributors | Open-source's exact word for people who give work without owning the project; The Labs is "Built in the open" under MIT and CC BY. | Plain; slightly technical. |
| Keepers, Regulars | "Keep the lights on"; "show up" is the brand kit's verb. | Cute; "regulars" is attendance, not contribution. |

Whatever the name, the pin's backstamp should drop the role word ("THE UPSKILLING LABS · 2026") so one object serves everyone. "Earned, not given." still holds. "Mentor" as an ambassador rank is retired: it is already a public Labs role (a registration option, an About-page card, first on Get Involved), and the guide tells ambassadors to invite experts to "guide a small team as a mentor."

## 3. Experience: the moments

1. **The launch page.** Hero and covers as they were. The launch section trades a decorative pulse for **the roster**: ten numbered slots for the founding ten, read from `data/roster.json` and empty at launch so every slot reads "Open". Real, honest, updatable like the schedule, and it turns "Recruiting our founding ambassadors" into something people can watch fill. The launch quote is now a real one: Suzie Zhang, Inaugural AI Cohort, from Technical.ly (confirm with her before launch).
2. **Orientation, in person.** Give first (film or the short read), then the coordinator's ten questions, then the agreement, then your name. Ten minutes. A coordinator beside you.
3. **The pass.** Built to be shown across a table. Then "Who's first?"
4. **The guide.** Home welcomes by name. Five steps end on "You're ready. Who's first?" with the button, not the pin, in the rail, on Home and in the done dialog.
5. **The coordinator's texts.** The site can't see who joins through whom, so the moments after "You're in" are the coordinator's to make. `/invite/` now carries them: the confirming invite for someone who finished on their own, "Someone just joined The Labs and said you sent them. That's one.", and "Your button's here." The second is the moment that keeps ambassadors ambassadors, and nothing produced it before.
6. **The pin.** One object for everyone who keeps The Labs running. Ambassadors earn it by passing it on.

**A design deviation, documented rather than fixed.** The brand kit says "We do not use all-caps on the website." The hero H1 is set in caps, and the 12px kickers are uppercase labels. The hero is treated as a product wordmark, the way a launch page sets a product name, and is now recorded as a deliberate exception in the README's design rules.

## Appendix A: The Labs, as it reads from outside

The alums of that pilot then founded The Upskilling Labs, in January 2026; DCPL's Chelsea Kirkland: "The folks that participated have gone on to create their own community led initiative that's called the Upskilling Labs."

A working board (Moscoso, Guzzi, Schmidt, Warren); "runs entirely on volunteer time." DC is "the flagship" with 184 members; waitlists in Baltimore, Dallas and Fairfax. Four Build Cycles a year, twelve weeks each; weekly free workshops; a Summit each cycle. Registration is Google sign-in through OLOS.
- **Its own best lines.** "Find your people. ## Appendix B: Red Antler's method, and the part to skip

Red Antler's method is a sequence, not an aesthetic: start from a human truth ("the most important section is not what the business does. It's what we want people to feel"); keep asking "but why does that matter?" until the answer is an emotion ("That's not a problem. That's a solution."); reframe the category by inverting its metric (Hinge "designed to be deleted", Ramp "Smart is the new platinum"); state the truth flatly and hang everything off it (Supergoop "There's no skincare without suncare"); say who you're not; give the brand a character with a built-in tension (USAFacts: "the passionate nerd… without convincing people to take sides"); write short declaratives in second person with the humour in the structure ("Best in bed", "Roll with it", "Furniture you want to move in with"); and for community brands make the audience the co-author (the Webbys' "We Made It"). The part to skip is the residue copycats turned into "blanding": the affable-but-not-overbearing voice, "we're passionate about", "thoughtfully sourced", "on a mission to", the founder-couldn't-find-a-good-one origin story. Heyward herself: "each time it's like a Xerox, it gets weaker and weaker." The test worth keeping, from GDUSA: "If I close my eyes, I should be able to identify the brand just by listening."

## Appendix C: What the first pass got wrong, and other findings

- **"Nobody finds it from an ad"** was an overclaim; the pilot had 200 applicants from DCPL's own channels. The line survives in the manifesto because it is true of what an ambassador does, not as a statement about all recruitment.
- **The covers.** The first pass proposed replacing them with "Ask. Share. Invite."; the second tried lines that carried the idea directly. Both were the wrong layer. The taglines are the page's framework, and the idea now lives inside the chapters as a prompt, not in place of them.
- **"You already know who"** presumed a name in the reader's head, and "do wonders for" promised too much. The prompt now describes the person ("figuring out what's next") and asks the reader to think of one.
- **"Almost there" is not permanent.** An invite link opened after finishing confirms the person on the spot (a code comment said so). The gap was that nobody was told; now the pending screen and `/invite/` both say it, and the coordinator's texts are written.
- **Provenance.** The ladder ("button at initiation, pin for Mentors") and the three taglines came with the mockup (`Main.dc.html`, commit a4b0d5c). The corps line was written in-repo (5457079). The guide's pin copy predates the mockup (be3e1df), so the conflict was staleness, not competing intent. The behavioural evidence the README leans on (the dated ask, retrieval practice, implementation intentions) is asserted, never cited; the "ruthless cuts" report is not in the repo.
- **The share link** counted ambassador applicants while the page promised a link that counts Upskillers (see "What waits on OLOS").
- **The done screen's message** dropped its closing parenthesis whenever the invited-by sentence was stripped; the template now keeps that sentence in the middle.
