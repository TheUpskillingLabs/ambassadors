## Brendan's direction so far

- **Role buttons:** Upskiller, Builder, Poderator, Mentor, Advisor, Ambassador.
  - Problem Owners are outside The Labs, so they don't get a role button.
  - Mentor is its own role (experts who help Pods). It's no longer the top of the ambassador track.
- **Two tiers in each workstream:**
  - **Tier 1, Trained:** the role button.
  - **Tier 2, Practitioner (or "practiced"):** a metal rim added to the role button (decided Sep 28, 2026, in place of a coin).
- **Challenge coins stay, for general use** (Sep 28, 2026). They're no longer the tier 2 marker. See "Challenge coins: moments and thanks".
- **Contributor is open to anyone,** in the open-source sense. It isn't a rank, and there's no gate.
- **The ladder** (set Sep 28, 2026): Contributor (open to anyone) → **Contributing Member** (the bare pin) → **Reviewer** → **Committer** → Maintainer, with **Supporting Member** alongside as the paid way in.
- **Membership has two doors** (Sep 28, 2026). It's a community status, not a statutory membership of the legal entity.
  - **Supporting Members** pay light dues, which help cover the cost of merch, and get a button.
  - **Contributing Members** are recognised for what they've contributed, and all of them wear the enamel pin.
    - **The bare pin is the Contributing Member pin (Brendan, Sep 28, 2026).** It has no formal requirement. At first it goes to the volunteers recognised for their early contributions, who couldn't go through the ranks because the ranks didn't exist yet.
    - **Reviewer is the first rank above it,** and adds the first ring. Committers and Maintainers add the second and third.
  - The two doors are separate. Paying dues isn't a step toward Reviewer, and a Reviewer doesn't have to pay. Someone can be both.
  - **No one buys a vote.** Following the YMCA, a vote has to be arranged, invited or consented to by the board, at the regional level and for the national org.
- **Structure (the YMCA model):** The Upskilling Labs (national) → The Upskilling Labs of [metro region], with its own governance separate from the software → neighbourhood Labs, which are branches of the regional org.
- **Neighbourhood Labs (Sep 28, 2026)** are small coordinating committees. Each has a small set of physical locations, which it activates in coordination with the regional workstream teams.
- **Merit ranks, as pins:**
  - **Contributing Member** (Sep 28, 2026): the enamel pin on its own. It's a recognition rather than a rank, and has no formal requirement.
  - **Reviewer** (confirmed Sep 28, 2026): the first merit rank, for people who have contributed helpfully in a number of roles. It's the pin with one ring.
    - Not "Peer": every paying Member is already one of The Labs' peers. Calling only the rank above them "Peer" would take their dues and then imply they aren't.
  - **Committer** (or The Labs' own word for it): has helped across all the core areas of The Labs. Committer comes with real permissions, because by then people have the experience and context.
  - **Maintainer / Steward:** the highest merit rank.
- **Owners:** the board and staff are the owners/authors. That's a governance role, not a merit rank.
- **Hiring (Sep 28, 2026):**
  - Each regional board hires an Executive Director, who can hire staff.
  - The national board hires the CEO.
- **The Fellowship** is a separate, paid and funded program.
- **Metal shows the level:** regional pieces (pins, coins) are brass, and national/HQ pieces are silver.
- **Merch:** all branded merch is ordered through HQ only.
- **Active vs emeritus, for every role:** each Lab or regional network is careful about how many people hold each role actively. Everyone else who has earned it is emeritus.

## How it starts: phasing (Brendan's direction, Sep 28, 2026)

- **One legal entity at first.** Regions start as steering committees inside The Upskilling Labs. The first is the Greater Washington Steering Committee.
- **The first CEO is effectively the ED for DC at first.**
- **National decisions:** the CEO decides, supported by an advisory committee and by the contributor community on national projects and tasks.
- **DC support:** the CEO supports the Greater Washington Steering Committee and hires a staff person to serve it directly. That lasts until the committee is largely self-organising and self-sustaining. Eventually it spins off entirely, as The Upskilling Labs of Greater Washington.
- **Over time,** the CEO and national staff focus more and more on expanding the network, and less on maintaining and growing DC.
- **The DC staff person also helps found other regional networks and Labs.** DC is where they start, not where they stay.

**Suggestions for making the handoff happen:**
- **Write down the spin-off criteria now.** Otherwise the first chapter tends to absorb the CEO indefinitely. Examples:
  - the steering committee runs a full Build Cycle without the CEO
  - DC's income covers its staff person
  - enough active Reviewers and Committers to fill its seats
  - the committee has practised governing the way its future board will (self-renewing or invited assembly)
  - DC has its own ED lined up, so the network builder can move on
- **Hire the DC staff person as a network builder, not DC's future ED.** They're national staff based in DC first, and they help found other regions.
  - **In DC, success means making themselves unnecessary.** They hand tasks to the steering committee and local volunteers.
  - **Everything they do in DC gets written down as the launch kit.** That covers founding a regional network (the steering committee, local partners, the crowdfunding campaign before a city's first Build Cycle) and founding a neighbourhood Lab (a venue, Poderators, the first Cycle). The kit is part of the commons, so each new region makes it better.
  - **Hire an organiser who has launched chapters and will travel,** not someone who wants to run one city's operations.
  - **Split their time on the books.** Their DC time comes out of DC's budget and their network time out of national's. Then DC learns to pay for its own staff before they move on.
  - **DC's own ED should be grown locally.** For example, someone from the steering committee or its Committers. Naming one becomes one of the spin-off criteria.
- **Keep DC's books separate from day one.** Run a DC program budget alongside the national budget, even inside one entity. That shows how much national time and money DC takes, and makes the spin-off a clean transfer rather than an untangling.
- **Let the advisory committee grow into the network assembly.** It could start with the Greater Washington chair and add each new region's chair and lead, which is how YMCA associations give input to their national board.
- **Tie the CEO's shift to milestones, not dates:**
  1. The DC staff person is hired, and the CEO steps back from day-to-day DC work.
  2. The committee runs a full Cycle, and the CEO attends only as an adviser.
  3. A second region is chartered, and DC's readiness to spin off is reviewed against the criteria.
- **Spin-off mechanics to plan for:**
  - separate incorporation, or coverage under a national group exemption
  - the charter, fair share and brand licence
  - transfer of contracts, staff, participant records and assets

## Fractal: regional forks and upstreaming

**Brendan's direction (Sep 28, 2026):**
- Regional orgs run and maintain localised instances or forks of the core software.
- The whole thing is fractal: many contributors to the national nonprofit tested their contributions at the neighbourhood or regional level first.

**Suggestions** (Brendan likes most of these, and the versioned API schema compatibility especially):
- **The same ladder repeats at every level, which answers the silver question.**
  - Each regional fork has its own Reviewers, Committers and Maintainers, in brass.
  - The national upstream has its own, in silver.
  - The path runs from proving something locally, to a regional rank, to contributing upstream, to a national rank.
- **Changes mature in stages, like Kubernetes features.** Kubernetes features move from alpha (off by default, experimental), to beta (tested, usually on), to GA (stable, supported). For The Labs:
  - **Pilot:** a neighbourhood Lab tries it.
  - **Regional:** the region adopts it.
  - **National:** it's upstreamed into the national standard.
  - A proposal to national says where the change was tested and what happened.
- **Keep forks close to upstream.** Forks that drift become unmaintainable, and the national platform can no longer connect to them. Chromium OS, for example, tracks the upstream Linux kernel, keeps its own patches "floated on top", and rebases every three to six months. The charter could require regional orgs to:
  - run a supported upstream version
  - keep OLOS's versioned API schema compatible, so national can gather network metadata (Brendan's favourite of these)
  - apply security fixes promptly
  - sync on a set schedule
  - make local changes through configuration or extensions wherever possible, rather than editing the core
- **Neighbourhood Labs don't fork.** They're coordinating committees, not software maintainers. They use their region's instance, and the pilot stage happens at their locations, run with the regional workstream teams.
- **Offer a hosted option.** Not every region will have Committers on the platform. National could host instances for regions without technical capacity. Self-managed forks are for regions that have the people.
- **Data stays local.** Participants' personal data stays in the regional instance, and national receives metadata only. The regional org is responsible for its own people's data.
- **Playbooks work the same way.** The CC BY playbooks fork, get piloted and are upstreamed exactly like the code.
- **Only the brand is restricted.** Anyone may fork the open-source code, but only a chartered org may call its instance "The Upskilling Labs of [metro region]".

## Neighbourhood Labs (suggestions)

A neighbourhood Lab is a small coordinating committee with a few physical locations. Regional workstream teams (Build Cycle, Learning Pathways, Community & Outreach, and so on) do the work, and the neighbourhood committee brings it to its places. That makes the region a grid: the workstream teams run across it, and the neighbourhood committees are fixed to places.

- **The committee works like a YMCA branch board.** Brendan's framing: neighbourhood Labs are to the regional org what YMCA branches are to their local association, and regional orgs are to national what local Ys are to YMCA of the USA. The YMCA shows two versions of the branch board:
  - **YMCA of Greater New York:** the main board appoints each branch's Board of Managers, 15 to 30 people. Branch boards plan programs, approve budgets and raise funds within the association's policies.
  - **YMCA of Metropolitan Milwaukee:** branch boards have 6 to 18 members on staggered three-year terms, and are "advisory in nature only," without the corporate powers of the main board.
  - **For The Labs:** the regional board or steering committee appoints each neighbourhood committee. The committee is advisory in corporate terms, but coordinates activation of its locations. It's small, with staggered terms, and drawn from Members and role-holders.
- **The regional org signs venue agreements.** The neighbourhood committee finds and activates locations, such as library branches or community centres, but contracts and partnerships stay with the regional org.
- **Pilots happen here.** A new idea is tried first at a neighbourhood Lab's locations, then adopted by the region, then upstreamed to national.
- **Seats follow locations.** The committee's size can scale with how many locations it activates, under the same active and emeritus rules as other roles.
- **The name follows the charter:** "[place] Lab", part of The Upskilling Labs of [metro region].

## Button, coin or pin: where the threshold sits (suggestion)

Brendan's instinct (Sep 28, 2026): neighbourhood committee members probably wear a pin. That needs a rule for when something is a button and when it becomes a pin.

**Cost isn't the dividing line.** At the volumes The Labs would order, a pin costs little more per piece than a button. The pin vendor research found printed metal pins from $1.99 each at 50. What differs:
- **Making it:** enamel needs a mould and takes 2–4 weeks. A button is printed paper under a clear cover, reprinted in days.
- **How long it lasts:** a button wears out in a season, while a pin lasts decades.
- **Reading it:** a button can be read across a room, so it speaks to the public. A pin is small and reads as insignia, so it speaks to people who know what it means.
- **Copying it:** anyone can print a button. A struck pin is hard to fake.

**The rule: a button says what you're doing; a pin says what The Labs has entrusted to you.** Something gets a pin only if all four of these hold:
1. **Conferred, not bought or signed up for.** It's earned (a merit rank) or given by a board (an appointed seat). No pin can be bought, the same principle as the vote.
2. **Lasting.** It means something for years: a permanent rank, or a term of office that leaves a record of service.
3. **Scarce.** The number of holders is capped by seats or limited by merit.
4. **On the register.** Every pin matches a numbered register entry.

If any one fails, it's a button. Tier 2 practice is shown by a metal rim added to the role button.

**How it sorts out:**

| Button (what you're doing) | Rim on the button (what you've practised) | Pin (what you've been entrusted with) | Coin (a moment or a thanks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upskiller, Poderator, Mentor, Ambassador | Practitioner in that role (a Builder is an Upskiller button with a brass rim) | **Merit:** Reviewer, Committer, Maintainer | Commemorative and thank-you coins; no status (see below) |
| Supporting Member (never gets a rim) | | **Office:** neighbourhood committee, regional steering committee or board, national advisory committee | |

**Tier 2 as a metal rim added to the button (Brendan's idea, Sep 28, 2026):** a Practitioner's own role button gets a metal rim or border added later, instead of (or as well as) a coin.

- **Two ways to make it:**
  - **A front ring sticker.** Electroformed nickel stickers are real metal about 0.08 mm thick, in silver or gold, peel-and-stick. They need no mould, and one maker's minimum is 10. A thin ring would sit on the face between the text and the edge, where there's about 2 mm of room. It's cheap, but it has to sit on a slightly domed face, it may lift at the edges, and it can't carry a number.
  - **A metal halo behind the button (suggested).** This is a laser-cut ring a little bigger than the button, taped to the tin back, so a band of metal shows all the way round.
    - Ponoko cuts and engraves brass up to 0.06" thick, with a smallest part of 15 × 15 mm. It starts at $50 for a single part and gets much cheaper at volume. SendCutSend cuts brass from 0.040" and can deburr and tumble, but doesn't engrave it.
    - For a 1.5" button, a halo of about 44 mm across would show roughly a 3 mm band of metal.
- **Why the halo:**
  - **It's real metal in the level's colour:** brass for regional, steel or nickel for national.
  - **It can be engraved with a number,** which matches the register entry.
  - **It moves to the next button.** When the role button is reissued, the metal stays and only the paper changes.
  - **It's visible,** so the public can spot an experienced Poderator or Mentor.
- **Check before cutting:**
  - The pin's hinge and clasp sit near the back's edge, so the ring may need two notches. Measure a real Sticker Mule button first.
  - Use strong double-sided tape, and deburr the edges.
  - Keep "certified" off it: it marks practice, not certification.
- **Consequences:**
  - **Decided:** the rim replaces the coin for tier 2. Coins stay, with a different job (see "Challenge coins: moments and thanks").
  - A Builder is an Upskiller button with a brass rim.
  - Supporting Member buttons never get a rim, because money doesn't earn practice.

**Consequences to decide:**
- **Advisors move from button to pin.** They're appointed to advise The Labs itself, so under this rule they hold an office.
- **Dues buy the button, never a pin.** A Supporting Member gets a dated member button. Pins stay out of reach of money.
- **Merit and office pins need different designs,** so the two can't be confused:
  - **Merit:** the orb family (relief, then glows, then cat-eye).
  - **Office:** a different shape, such as the swoosh chevron alone, with the Lab's or region's name engraved or on a small bar beneath. That's one shared mould; ask the vendor about engraving or bar attachments.
- **Office pins stay after the term.** Like a "past president" pin in a service club, the pin records the service. The register shows who holds the seat now, so the pin still isn't permission to act.
- **Metal still shows the level:** brass for neighbourhood and regional offices, silver for national.

## Positioning the enamel pin (suggestion, Sep 28, 2026)

The cat-eye enamel pin began as the reward for finishing the ambassador path. The system has since changed:
- Finishing the ambassador path now earns a brass rim on the Ambassador button (Ambassador Practitioner).
- The enamel pin now belongs to Contributing Members. On its own it's the Contributing Member pin, and rings show rank from Reviewer up.

**What the pin means now: recognised for contribution, never bought.**
- **The bare pin** says The Labs recognised the wearer's contribution to it. It's conferred, with no formal requirement.
- **The rings** are earned from peers. A Reviewer has practised in more than one part of The Labs, and two people who already hold the pin vouched for them.
- Nothing else in the system works this way: buttons can be paid for (Supporting Member) or handed out on training; office pins are appointed to a job; coins are thanks.

**The bare pin: Contributing Member (Brendan, Sep 28, 2026).**
- **What it is:** the Contributing Member pin, with no formal requirement. In the near future it goes mainly to volunteers recognised for their early contributions.
- **It solves the start-up problem.** Rings need vouching from people who already hold the pin, and at the start nobody does. Apache works the same way: a new project names its initial committers in its proposal, and meritocracy takes over from there.
- **Early volunteering counts as contribution.** Before The Labs had a commons, the people running its first programmes were also building its playbooks, kits and processes.
- **Keeping it meaningful without a formal requirement:** conferred, not claimed (the steering committee, or the ED/CEO for now, on a nomination); a one-line citation in the register; never sold; the founding cohort marked by register number and year on the back; the founding coin still says thanks to everyone who helped at launch.
- **Everyday wear:** ranked members can wear the bare pin alone. It understates their rank but never overstates it.

**Decided (Sep 28, 2026):**
- **Metal:** silver for national, brass for regional.
- **Rings are all the same width, and each rank adds one.** Every ring is 3 mm wide; the pin covers the centre out to 15.4 mm.

  | Rank | Rings | Across |
  |---|---|---|
  | Reviewer | 1 | about 37 mm |
  | Committer | 2 | about 43 mm |
  | Maintainer | 3 | about 49 mm |

- **The two steering committees get the first pins.** They're the first Contributing Members, with the bare pin.
- **Rank pins are normal one-piece pins, not stacks.** Each rank is its own pin, with its rings simulated in the metal; the fallback is the bare pin plus a single attachment carrying the rings.
  - Four designs: the bare pin (about 32 × 31 mm), then rank pins of about 37, 43 and 49 mm.
  - The first order is only the bare pin (the existing cat-eye design). The rank pins wait until the ranks start.
  - Keep the steps shallow (about 0.3 mm). A solid 49 mm pin is roughly 20–25 g: ask for a hollowed back, two posts and locking clutches.
  - Finish: a sunray (radial) brushed face with polished chamfers, engraved into the mould before plating. Confirm with the vendor.
  - Minimum orders are the catch: ask about small runs and splitting one order between brass and silver plating.
  - Promotion means a new pin; the wearer keeps the old one. An office is shown by its own office pin.

**Alternatives considered (superseded):** stacked rings behind the pin; a carved metal cockade with engraved tails; a pleated ribbon cockade (ink, teal and red bands, with printed tails); laser-cut backing plates; and a different design per rank. Renders and previews from Sep 28, 2026: `metal-cockades-tiers.png`, `cockade-sunray-tiers.jpg`, `pin-cockades.png`, `pin-rank-backings.png`, and the "Cockade" scene in `LabsPin.blend`.

**Metal finish:** polished brass for regional and polished nickel for national, so the cat-eye's raised lines catch the light. Polished brass reads as gold, which is fine as long as there's no gold tier.

**How to say it:** "Recognised, never bought." For the Reviewer ring: "Two practices. Two sponsors."

## Challenge coins: moments and thanks (suggestion)

**Brendan's direction (Sep 28, 2026):** the rim replaces the coin for tier 2, but The Labs still wants challenge coins in general.

**What coins are for:** they mark moments and say thanks. They carry no status, rights or rank, so unlike pins they can be given to anyone. That includes people outside The Labs, such as Problem Owners, library staff, partners and donors. It fits how coins are used elsewhere: fire and EMS departments issue them to mark anniversaries and milestones, to welcome recruits, and to present to community members.

**Suggested kinds:**
- **The founding coin.** Each regional network strikes one for its launch. It goes to:
  - the backers of the crowdfunding campaign that comes before a city's first Build Cycle;
  - its founding volunteers;
  - its first partners.

  The network builder collects one from every region they help found.
- **The Cycle coin.** One per Build Cycle, handed out at Demo Day to everyone who took part, and to the Problem Owners who brought the problems.
- **The leader's coin.** The CEO (silver) and each regional ED (brass) hand these out, at their own discretion, as personal thanks. Armed forces commanders' coins work the same way.
- **The visit coin.** Given when people from one Lab visit or help another, so coins travel around the network.

**Rules that keep coins from blurring into status:**
- **Coins say what they commemorate,** never a rank or role.
- **Coins can reach donors.** A founding coin for crowdfunding backers is fine, because coins aren't status. Pins stay beyond the reach of money.
- **Metal follows the level:** brass for regional coins, silver for national.
- **Limited runs can be numbered.** Coin makers commonly offer sequential engraved numbering.
- **Keep designs few, because each one has a mould fee.** One vendor charges $100–250 per order by size, and waives it from 300 coins. A shared front with a different back for each region would save on moulds, if the vendor allows it; that's still unconfirmed.
- **Optional tradition:** some departments hold "coin checks," challenging members to produce their coin. It's a light ritual that keeps people carrying them.

## Membership and dues (suggestion)

**The full ladder is now almost exactly the CNCF contributor-ladder template:**

| | Contributor | Supporting Member | Reviewer | Committer | Maintainer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The Labs** | open to anyone | light dues, a button, a separate door | the pin with one ring (Contributing Member starts with the bare pin) | pin with two rings, real permissions | pin with three rings, top rank |
| **CNCF template equivalent** | Contributor | Organization Member (earned there, not paid) | Reviewer | (none) | Maintainer |

**How membership works elsewhere (researched Sep 28, 2026):**

| Organisation | How you become a member | Dues |
|---|---|---|
| Apache Software Foundation | Nominated and elected by existing members, for merit | None; membership is merit-only |
| GNOME Foundation | Any contributor to GNOME | None listed |
| Kubernetes (Member) | Active contributor, sponsored by two reviewers | None |
| Python Software Foundation | Basic: sign up. Supporting: pay. Contributing: 5+ hours a month volunteering, self-certified. Fellow: nominated | Basic free (no vote); Supporting $99 a year on a sliding scale; Contributing and Fellow free |
| Open Source Initiative | Sign up at a tier | Basic free (no vote); Supporting $50 a year (can vote for and run for the board); Professional $300 a year |
| Drupal Association | Choose a tier | From $0 (complimentary or honorary) to $50+ a month; the entry tier includes voting rights and a digital badge |
| ACM | Pay | $99 a year for professionals, $19 for students, $50 for financial hardship, $74 for retired members |
| Rotary | Join a club | Rotary International's share is $82 a year (2025–26), rising to $85.50 in 2026–27; districts and clubs add their own dues |
| Toastmasters | Join a club | $72 every six months from Aug 3, 2026 ($144 a year), plus a one-time $25 new-member fee |
| HacDC (DC hackerspace) | Pay dues and attend a monthly member meeting | $60 a month, with a waiver "only in extreme circumstances"; includes 24/7 key access and a vote at meetings |
| Code for America brigades | Volunteer in a local chapter | Free. Code for America fiscally sponsored about 60 chapters, then ended the network in 2023 |

**What this means for The Labs:**
1. **There are three patterns.**
   - Merit membership is free (Apache, GNOME, Kubernetes).
   - Supporter membership is paid, but nearly always sits beside a free route (PSF, OSI, Drupal).
   - Clubs, professional bodies and makerspaces charge everyone (Rotary, Toastmasters, ACM, HacDC).
   - The Labs' Member (paid, with Reviewer and above earned) fits the supporter pattern.
2. **"Light" means $50 a year or less.** That's at or below OSI's $50 and well below PSF and ACM ($99), Rotary ($82+) and Toastmasters ($144 plus $25). It's also under the IRS's $75 disclosure line.
3. **Wherever there are dues, there's usually a way in without paying:**
   - PSF: volunteer hours, or its sliding scale.
   - Drupal: the $0 complimentary tier.
   - ACM: the $50 hardship rate.
   - OSI: the free Basic tier.
   - HacDC is the strict end, with a waiver only in extreme circumstances.
   - This supports a sliding scale plus a no-questions waiver for The Labs.
4. **Elsewhere, paying members usually get a vote for the board** (OSI's $50 tier, PSF, Drupal, HacDC). The Labs' Members won't, because the membership isn't statutory. So say up front what Members do get:
   - merch they keep
   - their entry in the register
   - events
   - a voice in program decisions
   Otherwise dues can feel like paying for a vote that isn't there.
5. **Dues can be split between HQ and the regions.** Rotary splits dues between the international body, districts and clubs. The Labs could split between HQ (merch, the register) and regional Labs (events).
6. **Free volunteer chapters are fragile.** Code for America ended its brigade network in 2023. It cited falling volunteerism, privacy and security concerns with volunteers partnering with governments, and short projects that couldn't have a lasting impact. Dues don't fix those problems, but they give each chapter some income of its own and ask members for a small commitment.

**Member for the dues tier (settled), with Reviewer above it.**
- Dues and "member" go together: Rotary, Toastmasters and the Python Software Foundation all charge members dues. "Paying to be a peer" reads oddly.
- The cost is renaming today's sign-ups, who are currently counted as "members" (183 on the site, 300+ in the deck). Call them the community instead, for example "a community of 300+", and keep "Member" for people who have joined formally.

**Not statutory.** Under DC's nonprofit law, a "member" is someone with the right to vote for directors or on fundamental transactions.
- The same law says a nonprofit doesn't have to have members.
- The bylaws can call people "members" without making them members under the Act. Those people then have only the rights the bylaws give them.
- So the bylaws should say plainly that The Labs has no statutory members, and list what Members do get.
- This assumes The Labs incorporates in DC. The lawyer drafting the bylaws should confirm it for whichever state it incorporates in.

**Structure and voting: the YMCA model (Brendan's direction, Sep 28, 2026)**

Brendan prefers how the YMCA does it:
- **National:** The Upskilling Labs.
- **Regional:** The Upskilling Labs of [metro region]. Each regional org has its own governance, separate from the software.
- **Neighbourhood:** neighbourhood Labs, which are branches of the regional org: small coordinating committees, each activating a few physical locations with the regional workstream teams.
- **No bought votes.** Paying dues doesn't buy a vote. A vote has to be arranged, invited or consented to by the board.

**How the YMCA works (researched):**

| YMCA | The Labs |
|---|---|
| The National Council of YMCAs (YMCA of the USA). It owns the name and marks: "the name 'Young Men's Christian Association' and all names, marks, logos… are the property of the National Council" | The Upskilling Labs (national) |
| Local associations, such as the YMCA of Greater New York or of Metropolitan Washington. There are about 2,700 separate organisations with about 10,000 branches | The Upskilling Labs of [metro region] |
| Branches, such as the Anthony Bowen YMCA in DC | Neighbourhood Labs: coordinating committees with a few locations, not boards |

- **What a local association owes the national:**
  - It pays "one-twelfth of its share of the financial support" to the National Council each month.
  - It certifies every year that it accepts the statement of purpose and doesn't unlawfully discriminate.
  - Its chief executive must meet qualifications set by the National Board.
- **The national board elects itself.** Nominees from its Board Development Committee are "elected by the National Board." Local associations give input through Association Assemblies, which each association joins through its chief volunteer officer and chief executive.
- **Local members pay fees but don't vote.** Voting is set up separately, in one of two ways:
  - **An invited assembly (YMCA of Greater New York).** The voting members are the General Assembly: the directors, plus representatives each branch's Board of Managers appoints every year from its branch's members. The Assembly elects the at-large directors and can amend the constitution. The branch boards themselves are appointed by the main board.
  - **No voting members (YMCA of Metropolitan Milwaukee).** Its bylaws create no voting membership, so the board picks its own successors. Its branch boards are "advisory in nature only."

**What this means for The Labs:**
- **Supporting and Contributing Members stay, without votes.** Supporting Members fund The Labs. Contributing Members are everyone who holds the pin: the bare pin, and the Reviewers, Committers and Maintainers above it. Neither kind buys a vote.
- **Each regional board picks one of the two YMCA routes:**
  - **A self-renewing board (Milwaukee).** This is the simplest. There are no statutory members, so there are no member meetings or quorums.
  - **An invited assembly (New York).** The regional board, plus representatives each neighbourhood Lab's coordinating committee appoints, elect some of the directors. Under DC law these invited voting members would be statutory members, with the rights that come with that:
    - removing the directors they elected
    - approving dissolution
    - quorum at member meetings

    Because the board controls who is invited, the group stays small and engaged, and no one can buy their way in.
- **The national board elects itself, with input from the regions.** That input comes through an assembly of each regional org's board chair and lead, as in the YMCA.
- **Hiring follows the same split.** Regional boards hire their Executive Directors, who hire regional staff. The national board hires the CEO.
  - The regional ED and the national CEO have no reporting line between them. The charter connects them, and national staff support the regions, the way YMCA of the USA supports local Ys.
  - As the YMCA does for local chief executives, the charter could require regional EDs to meet qualifications set by national. The regional board still makes the hire.
- **Money flows up.** Members pay their dues to their regional org. Each regional org pays a set share to national, the YMCA's "fair share", which covers the standards, the platform, and merch bought through HQ.
- **Brand approval becomes the charter.** National owns the name and marks. A regional org uses "The Upskilling Labs of [metro region]" because it's a chartered member of the network. It keeps that right by certifying every year that it meets national standards: the purpose, non-discrimination, and qualifications for its lead.
- **Two governance tracks:**
  - **The organisation:** the boards run programs, money, people and the brand locally.
  - **The commons:** Contributor → Contributing Member → Reviewer → Committer → Maintainer govern the software and playbooks. They're merit-based and national, and carry no organisational vote.
  - So Committers' program permissions (running a Build Cycle, approving local playbook changes) come from the regional board. Permissions over the commons come from national.
- **Legal set-up.** YMCA associations are separate nonprofits. Today The Labs' regional work sits inside one legal entity. If regional orgs become separate nonprofits, the national org's 501(c)(3) could hold an IRS group exemption that covers them, so each doesn't have to apply individually.

**Dues buy belonging, not rank.**
- Paying makes you a Supporting Member. Contributing membership starts with the bare pin, conferred for contribution, so it can't be bought.
- Dues should never gate taking part (being an Upskiller) or contributing.

**Keep membership open to people who can't pay.**
- The Python Software Foundation offers two routes to voting membership: a $99 annual fee with a sliding scale, or at least 5 hours a month of volunteering, self-certified.
- The Labs' version could be light dues on a sliding scale, plus a no-questions-asked waiver. That matters for a free program serving people who've lost work.

**What dues cover:**
- The member's button and entry in the register.
- The rims and pins others earn, so every piece is paid for before it's awarded.
- To set the amount, start from real quotes: the expected pieces per member each year, times the unit cost, plus shipping.

**Money and tax:**
- While The Labs is fiscally sponsored by Superbloom, dues would flow through Superbloom. Check the sponsorship agreement allows dues and merch.
- Dues that buy merch aren't a pure donation. The IRS requires a written disclosure for any payment over $75 where the donor gets something back.
- Keeping dues at $75 a year or less keeps things simple. Confirm with Superbloom or an accountant.

**Lapsed membership (option):**
- Membership renews each year. Ranks are permanent, so a lapsed membership doesn't take a rank away.
- If active seats require a current membership, a lapse moves someone to emeritus, and renewing brings them back.

## The volunteer community and the contributor community (suggestion)

**The core difference: volunteers give time to The Labs' programs, and contributors give work to The Labs' commons.** Volunteers work *in* the program: running a workshop, shepherding a Pod, talking to a room. Contributors work *on* the system: a playbook, a kit, OLOS code, research.

| | Volunteer community | Contributor community |
|---|---|---|
| What they give | Time, in a role | Work, as something others can reuse |
| Where it happens | Live, in public, in The Labs' name | In the open commons, on their own schedule |
| Where the gate sits | On the person: training, conduct and vetting, before they act | On the work: it's reviewed before it's accepted |
| Who can join | Anyone who's onboarded, up to the seats a Lab needs | Anyone, any time, from anywhere |
| What limits its size | Seats | How many Reviewers there are to review work |
| Who it belongs to | The Labs: volunteers represent it | Everyone: Apache-2.0 for code, CC BY 4.0 for docs |
| What they agree to | A volunteer agreement (conduct, safeguarding, use of the brand) | Licence terms for their contributions |
| Recognition | Buttons and rims: what you do and have practised | Credit, then the Contributing Member pin, then ranks: Reviewer → Committer → Maintainer |
| Pay | Unpaid by definition | Doesn't matter: staff, fellows and outsiders all contribute |

**Why the gate sits in different places.** A workshop can't be reviewed before it happens, so The Labs has to trust the person first. A playbook edit can be reviewed, so anyone can propose one. That's why open source can be open to everyone: review absorbs the risk.

**Practice is the bridge between them.**
- Tiers measure practice in the volunteer community. Ranks measure trust over the commons.
- You can only review a playbook for work you've done yourself, which is why Reviewer requires being a Practitioner in two workstreams.
- The step up from volunteer to contributor is turning what you learned while delivering into something others can reuse.

**What follows from this:**
1. **Seat caps apply to volunteer roles and permissions, never to contributing.** The contributor community's real limit is review capacity, so Reviewers are where to invest.
2. **Every workstream has a live side and a reusable side, so tier 2 criteria should count both.**
   - Outreach isn't just volunteering. Ambassadors also make flyers, slide decks, scripts and the playbooks Labs projects use to grow their own communities, and all of that is contribution.
   - The Build Cycle and Learning Pathways work the same way: you deliver, then write what you learned back into the playbooks.
   - Platform (OLOS) is the exception, almost all contribution: accepted work is the practice.
3. **The contributor community is bigger than The Labs.** Other organisations can adopt and improve the playbooks, which is how the method spreads.
   - The licences allow this. Neither passes on the brand: Apache 2.0 (section 6) and CC BY 4.0 both exclude trademark rights.
   - So others can run the method, but not as The Labs. The trademark policy keeps that line.
4. **Contributions need licence terms coming in.**
   - GitHub's terms make contributions to a licensed repo "inbound=outbound": you license what you add under the repo's licence.
   - Many projects also ask for a DCO sign-off, a line in each commit certifying the contributor has the right to submit it.
   - Handbook edits made outside GitHub need the same terms.
5. **Watch for a two-class community.** Volunteers do the hard, in-person work. If prestige flows only to the commons ranks, they'll feel second-class. Tiers, rims and emeritus titles are the volunteer community's recognition, so keep them as visible as the pins.
6. **A possible answer to the silver question:** brass ranks mean trust over a Lab's local practice and playbooks, and silver ranks mean trust over the national commons.

## Brand use by regional Labs and communities

**Brendan's direction:** like a university's brand guidelines, regional orgs and communities are free to use the design system, but the national/HQ brand, logo and name can't be used without approval.

**How others do it:**
- **University of Minnesota:**
  - Registered student organisations may use two specific marks made for student groups, "in their entirety, without alteration".
  - A group can be the "Gardening Club at the University of Minnesota", but not the "UMN Gardening Club".
  - Every group must state that it "is independent from the University of Minnesota".
- **University of Missouri:** student groups may use any colours, but may not put the university's protected marks in their logos.
- **TEDx:**
  - Organisers get their event logo from TED's generator, with fixed colour, font and layout.
  - "Never use the TED or TEDx logos!" Only the full, approved event name (TEDx plus a place) can be used.
- **Rotary:**
  - Only clubs, districts and official Rotary entities may use the name and logos.
  - Every project name includes the club's name, so the affiliation reads as the club's, not Rotary International's.
  - Club logos come from the Brand Center's template and can't be altered.

**Suggested: three layers for The Labs**
1. **The design system is open to regional Labs and communities.** No approval is needed to use it. It covers:
   - colours and Geologica
   - layout and components
   - photography and illustration style
   - voice and the glossary
   - templates
2. **Each regional org and neighbourhood Lab gets an identity issued by HQ.** Following the YMCA model, the regional org is "The Upskilling Labs of [metro region]", and neighbourhood Labs are named for their place under it. HQ makes the lockups from a template, like a TEDx or Rotary club logo. The regional org uses them because it's a chartered member of the network.
3. **The national marks are HQ only.** These are:
   - The Upskilling Labs and The Labs as names
   - the orb mark and wordmark
   - pins, coins and merch

   Regional Labs use them only through HQ-issued lockups and pre-approved templates. Anything else goes to HQ for approval.

**Keeping approval from becoming a bottleneck:**
- **Pre-approved templates, with the brand areas locked.** Decks, flyers, social tiles, signage and event pages used as they are need no approval; only changes to the locked areas go to HQ. The ambassador deck (a boilerplate plus a customisable template) is the first case.
- **Collateral flows back to the commons.** Flyers and decks made by ambassadors and regional Labs are contributions. HQ reviews each one once, and the approved ones join a shared template library that every Lab can use.
- **A published turnaround time for approvals.** Without one, people route around the process.
- **Reserved signature elements, not just the logo.** The ink field with teal and red glows is effectively part of the orb mark. Decide which elements stay reserved (for example the orb, the swoosh chevron and the glow or cat-eye treatment), so that regional work built from the design system doesn't read as national.
- **A naming rule:** regional orgs take the fixed form "The Upskilling Labs of [metro region]", like "YMCA of Metropolitan Washington". This replaces the earlier idea of keeping the national name out of regional names. National still owns the name, and no other form is allowed.
- **Clear terms for adopters outside the network.** Anyone using the open playbooks already has two obligations under CC BY 4.0. It requires them to credit The Labs as the source. It also forbids implying they're "connected with, or sponsored, endorsed, or granted official status by" The Labs. The trademark policy covers the logo and name beyond that.
- **Revocable approval.** The charter, and with it the name and lockups, can be withdrawn if a regional org stops certifying that it meets national standards.
- **A split brand kit.** The public brand style guide currently links the orb mark and logo lockup for download alongside everything else. Split it into "Design system: for the network" and "National marks: HQ only".

## Naming the rank below Committer (research)

Open-source projects keep "contributor" open to anyone, then put a recognised rung between it and commit access:

| Project | Open to anyone | Recognised rung(s) before commit access | Commit-level role |
|---|---|---|---|
| CNCF contributor ladder template | Community Participant, Contributor | Organization Member (two sponsors), then Reviewer | Maintainer |
| Kubernetes | contributors | Member ("continuously active", two sponsors), then Reviewer | Approver |
| Node.js | contributors ("both collaborators and non-collaborators may propose changes") | Triager | Collaborator |
| Python | contributors | Triager | Core developer |
| Debian | Contributor (a Developer sponsors the work) | Debian Maintainer (can upload their own packages, with restrictions) | Debian Developer |
| Mozilla | contributors | Module Peer (appointed by the module owner) | Module Owner |
| Apache | Contributor ("developer"), no formal recognition | none | Committer |

**Reviewer was chosen.** It's the rung just below approval in Kubernetes and the CNCF template, where reviewers are trusted to review work in the areas they know. It makes the ladder say what each rank is trusted to do:
- Contributors: anyone.
- Reviewers: review work in the workstreams they've practised.
- Committers: approve it.
- Maintainers: set direction.

**Names ruled out for the Reviewer rank:**
- **Member:** The Labs already uses "member" for everyone who signs up. It's now the dues tier below Reviewer (see "Membership and dues").
- **Collaborator:** in Node.js it means commit access, and on GitHub it means anyone added to a repo.
- **Triager:** too technical outside software.
- **Peer:** Mozilla's term. It's warm, but every Member is already a peer, so using it for a rank above Members would suggest they aren't.

**How people qualify (from the CNCF template and Kubernetes):**
- Two sponsors who already hold the rank. Those projects require the sponsors to come from different employers; The Labs' version could be different workstreams or different Labs, which keeps the rank from being handed out within one clique.
- Contributions that have been checked, and a minimum time active.

**Crediting open contributors:** they can get credit without a gate, for example a public list of everyone who has contributed, the way All Contributors does it in repos.

  - Pins mark what you've been entrusted with.
  - Coins mark moments and thanks.
- **Superseded: tier 2 as a challenge coin.** The rim does this job now, and it's visible in public. "Two rims, and two sponsors, earn the brass Reviewer pin" works the same way.
- **Reviewer = Practitioner in two workstreams, plus two sponsors.** For example, you've been a Builder and given back in one more role.
- **Practitioners help train newcomers** in their workstream. The training and the pass bar stay central (see the guardrails below).
- **Rank shown by design, since metal now shows the level** (possibly superseded; see "Positioning the enamel pin", which suggests one pin with rank marks):
  - Reviewer: the orb in relief, with one enamel colour.
  - Committer: the full mark with the teal and red glows.
  - Maintainer: the cat-eye with glitter.
  - The existing cat-eye pin (polished nickel) would be the national Maintainer pin, and a brass version the regional one.

## Guardrails: keeping control of standards

The rule: **ranks recognise, active seats carry the permissions, and the board sets what those permissions are.** Pins record what someone has done. A register of active roles says what they can do now.

1. **Committer permissions.** Committers get real permissions. To keep the boards in charge, a board grants them once, in writing, to every active Committer as a group, not case by case. Following the YMCA model, program permissions come from the regional board and permissions over the commons come from national. The grant lists what's included. The board can amend or revoke it, and it lapses when a Committer goes emeritus. A draft of what the grant covers:
   - **Active Committers can:**
     - Approve changes to their Lab's playbooks within the national standard.
     - Sign off Practitioners in the workstreams they've practised.
     - Run a Build Cycle or a workshop series without approval for each event.
     - Confirm awards in the register for their Lab.
   - **These stay with HQ and the board:**
     - The brand, and anything public-facing that departs from it.
     - The national standard and the criteria for tiers and ranks.
     - Money, contracts and partnerships.
     - Conduct cases.
2. **Maintainers:** Maintainers nominate and the board confirms. No rank carries a governance vote. (At Apache, the top merit rank elects the board; The Labs shouldn't copy that.)
3. **Training:** Practitioners sponsor and coach newcomers, but the central training and pass bar do the sign-off. The workstream owner spot-checks.
4. **Permanence:** a conduct clause allows removal from the record, since even Apache says merit can be lost for breaking community ethics. A physical pin can't be taken back, so it's never permission to act for The Labs; the register is.
5. **Awards come before objects:** an award is recorded first. The handshake is the ceremony, not the decision.
6. **What the public reads into a button:** don't let a button suggest vetting or certification The Labs hasn't done. Check host libraries' volunteer rules, avoid "certified," and describe the tiers as community recognition.
7. **Builder:** it follows a confirmed, named contribution to a problem owner, not attendance at Demo Day. Who confirms it is still open.
8. **Chapters:** the criteria live in the national repo. Chapters nominate, and HQ records.

## Active seats and emeritus

- **The rank is permanent; the permissions come with an active seat.** Seats are limited.
  - Emeritus holders keep the pin, the title (for example, "Poderator emeritus") and their voice: they can still propose and review.
  - They don't hold the permissions, and they don't wear the role button.
- **Seat numbers follow the work, not a fixed quota:**
  - **Poderators:** one per Pod in the current cycle.
  - **Mentors:** a set ratio to Pods.
  - **Ambassadors:** the planned corps size (about 60 by month 6 in the launch plan).
  - **Committers:** at least two for every core area, so no area depends on one person, and no more than the area needs.
  - **Maintainers and Advisors:** a small, fixed number per area.
- **Terms:**
  - Seats are reconfirmed at the end of each Build Cycle.
  - Someone inactive for a set period moves to emeritus automatically. Kubernetes, for comparison, removes members after 12 months with no contributions. The CNCF template leaves the period to each project and offers stepping down or emeritus status as the voluntary routes.
- **Coming back:** an emeritus holder can take an open seat without qualifying again.
- **Who sets the numbers:** HQ sets the sizing rules nationally, the regional ED and staff fill each Lab's seats, and the regional board approves exceptions.
- **Watch-out: caps can feel like gatekeeping.**
  - Make emeritus an honour: it's how the rank is kept, not lost.
  - Publish each Lab's seat counts and how they're set.
  - Keep a clear line to the next open seat.
- **Option:** print the cycle or year on buttons for time-bound roles (Poderator, Mentor, Upskiller), so a button shows it's current.

## Merch through HQ

- **Awarding and ordering are separate.** Chapters record awards in the register. HQ makes and ships only what the register shows, so every order doubles as an audit.
- **Stock:**
  - Chapters hold a stock of role buttons, because initiations happen weekly.
  - Pins and coins ship from HQ for each award.
- **Numbering:** number the permanent pieces so each one matches a register entry. Sequential engraved numbering is a common option for coins. Ask the pin factory about numbering pins on the back.
- **Pooled orders:** ordering for every chapter at once reaches volume price breaks sooner. For example, one coin maker waives its mould fee from 300 coins and keeps moulds for two years for reorders.
- **Policy, not hidden files:**
  - Control of the brand comes from a trademark and merch policy. Apache licenses its code freely, but says the licence doesn't grant use of its marks, and asks anyone making merch beyond personal use for permission.
  - The Labs needs a one-page version: only HQ produces merch with The Labs' marks.
  - Whether to register the marks is a question for a lawyer.
- **Print files:** the print-ready button files are in `design/button/` in the ambassadors repo, which is public on GitHub. Today anyone can download them and order the button.
  - Moving the print files somewhere private stops new copies.
  - Files already committed stay in the repo's git history unless that history is rewritten.
- **Budget:** decide whether HQ pays up front or chapters reimburse HQ at cost.
