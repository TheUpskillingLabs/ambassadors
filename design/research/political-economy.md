# What it would take: The Labs as a nonpartisan force in Washington

> A strategy note, Sep 29, 2026, for Brendan and the board. The question: what would it take, in political-economy terms, for The Labs to become a potent socio-economic and sociotechnical presence in Congress and presidential affairs while staying strictly nonpartisan and never becoming an advocacy organization. The vision it starts from: summits in DC where teams fly in from metros across the country, and senators and representatives come to see their own constituents' work and learn what the network is finding on the frontier of the future of work. Not approved by anyone. Builds on `recognition-system.md`, `expansion-metros.md` and `structure-diagram.md`.

## The one idea: Cooperative Extension for the age of AI

The most potent nonpartisan, place-based, hands-on learning network the United States ever built is the Cooperative Extension Service. The Smith-Lever Act of 1914 put an extension agent in nearly every county, tied to a land-grant university, funded by federal, state and county money together, and gave it one job: bring the frontier of knowledge to the people doing the work, and bring what they learned back. 4-H grew out of it. A century later it still has agents in most counties, a youth program that brings delegates to Washington every year, and a standing claim on both parties, because every member of Congress has extension agents in their district and nobody thinks of them as political.

That is the frame that makes the vision coherent. The Labs is not a think tank, a lobby, a training provider or a movement. It is the extension service for the future of work: a network that learns by building in neighborhoods, writes down what it learns in a commons, and carries it upstream. Everything below follows from that frame, and it is the frame to say out loud in Washington, because it tells a senator in one sentence what The Labs is and why it isn't a threat.

## Where potency comes from, when you never ask for anything

An organization that refuses to lobby or endorse has five sources of influence, and the summit is the place where all five meet.

**1. Constituents in the room.** The only thing every member of Congress reliably shows up for is their own voters doing something admirable. The Congressional App Challenge works on exactly this: each member hosts it in their district, winners are shown in the Capitol, and members attend because the students are theirs. The National League of Cities and the Conference of Mayors fly local officials to Washington every March and get delegation meetings because the visitors are the people who run the member's home town. The Labs' version is stronger than either, because the visitors are not officials or winners but ordinary constituents showing a working thing they built for a real local problem. The rule that makes it work: every team invites every member whose district it touches, and the invitation is identical regardless of party. A team from a metro whose county sits in three districts invites three members, and that is the whole political statement about fractured representation. Nobody has to say the word.

**2. Knowledge nobody else has.** The network will know things about work at the neighborhood level that no agency, consultancy or university knows, because it will have thousands of people building with AI tools in libraries in Flint and Greensboro and Las Vegas and writing down what happened. Turned into one annual report with a plain name, released at the summit, that becomes the thing staffers cite. The Labs' credibility here depends on never editorializing: what we saw, what worked, what didn't, with the numbers. The model is the National Academies, not a think tank. Congressional offices trust the source that never has an ask.

**3. People.** Influence in Washington is who is in the room when the memo is written. TechCongress places technologists as congressional staff; the Presidential Innovation Fellows do the same in the executive branch. A Labs fellowship that places Maintainers and Approvers, people who have seen every part of the network up close, in congressional offices and agencies for a year is the single highest-leverage thing the network can do after the summit exists. Within five years there are Labs alumni writing workforce language in both parties' offices, and they don't need to be asked to think of the network.

**4. The convening itself.** An annual event that members plan around becomes an institution. The design matters more than the size: it has to be a demonstration, never a lobby day. No asks, no scorecards, no bills. Members come to see, listen, and be photographed with their constituents' work. The host tells them what the network learned this year. Then they leave. The discipline of never asking is what makes them come back, and it is what keeps the event legal and clean under section 501(c)(3), where educating officials on invitation is fine and electioneering is fatal.

**5. Being the delivery network.** Every administration, of either party, wants a workforce agenda and lacks a way to deliver it to neighborhoods. The executive orders on AI education and skilled trades in 2025, the short-term Pell expansion that took effect in 2026, state workforce boards, the community college system: all of them need somewhere for a person to actually go on a Tuesday night. A network with a Lab in a library in a hundred metros is the delivery system for whichever agenda is in power, and that is the most durable position there is, because it does not depend on who wins. The trap is becoming a grantee. Federal money makes the network a program, and programs get cut. The network's core has to run on dues, regional fair-share and earned revenue, with federal money taken only at the regional level for specific projects and never for the commons.

## The summit, designed to be potent

**When.** Late March, the week the National League of Cities is in town, or the week of National Library Week in April. Congress is in session, appropriations are being written, and the Hill is used to fly-ins.

**Where.** The Library of Congress. The Labs started in a library; the summit belongs in the nation's. The Jefferson Building's great hall for the exhibition, the Coolidge Auditorium for the report, and the Madison Building for the delegation meetings. If the Library says no in year one, the Rayburn foyer and the Kennedy Caucus Room are the standard fallbacks, and either House Administration or the Rules Committee of the Senate will need a member sponsor for the room, which is itself a bipartisan invitation to extend.

**What happens.**
- **The exhibition.** Every metro brings one team and one thing they built, staged the way a science fair is staged: the thing, the problem owner it was built for, and the people. Teams stand by their work. Members walk the floor with a printed map showing which teams come from their district, and the network's staff walk them to those teams first.
- **The delegation visits.** Each team spends one afternoon in the offices of every member whose district it touches, showing the work on a laptop. This is not lobbying; it is show and tell, and it is what the App Challenge does with fifteen-year-olds.
- **The report.** One hour, one speaker, the network's findings from the year: what people in a hundred neighborhoods built, what tools they used, what they couldn't do and why, what jobs they got. Released the same morning, with the data.
- **The pinning.** The year's Reviewers, Approvers and Maintainers are pinned on the Hill, by their sponsors, in front of their members. A member who pins a constituent's ring on stage has joined the network's story without endorsing anything.
- **What is missing.** No asks, no policy platform, no panels with lobbyists, no sponsor logos from companies that sell to government. The absence is the design.

**Who co-chairs.** Two former members, one from each party, as honorary co-chairs of the summit and of a standing advisory council. Former members are free to be nonpartisan in a way sitting members are not, and they know which staff to call.

## The sequence

- **Year one (DC).** A small Demo Day on the Hill for the DC region only: the Maryland and Virginia delegations, the House Future of Work Caucus, the DC delegate, and the Library of Congress as the venue ask. The point is to prove the format, take the photographs, and get one member to say "do this again."
- **Years two and three (the first ten metros).** The first national summit, with every metro represented. The annual report begins. The first two fellows go to the Hill. The advisory council forms around the co-chairs.
- **Years three to five (a hundred metros).** A member-hosted "Labs Challenge" in each district, on the App Challenge model, so every member has a reason to visit their local Lab once a year at home. State chapters begin working with governors and state workforce boards, which is where most workforce policy is actually written. The fellowship reaches the executive branch. A White House convening, in the tradition of the 2014 Maker Faire, becomes possible under either party.
- **The long game.** A congressional charter under Title 36, like 4-H's home in the Department of Agriculture, is the formal version of being the extension service. Congress has granted almost no new charters in thirty years, so it is a prestige goal, not a plan. The realistic version is a line in the next reauthorization of the workforce law that names community learning labs as an allowable use of state funds, which the network never asks for and which a fellow, an alumnus, or a member who has been to the summit writes on their own.

## The rules that keep it clean

- **Symmetry is a law, not a courtesy.** Every invitation, every visit, every photograph, every co-chair pairing is symmetric by party. A Lab in a district represented by a member the board dislikes gets the same visit.
- **No asks, ever, from the c3.** Educating officials at their invitation and on the network's own findings is permitted; requesting action is not the network's business. If the board ever wants advocacy, it builds a separate c4 with separate money and a separate name, the way AARP and the Sierra Club do, and it keeps the summit out of it.
- **The data is the argument.** The network publishes what it finds, including what embarrasses it. The day it shades a finding to please a member is the day it becomes a lobby.
- **Metro selection is about voice, not maps.** The expansion research picks places where representation is fractured, and that stays an internal analysis. The public reason for every metro is the same: people there had the least access to the frontier of work, and a library said yes.
- **No federal money in the commons.** Regional projects may take grants; the national commons, the register and the summit run on dues, fair-share and earned revenue.
- **Members see constituents, not staff.** The network's staff introduce and step back. The constituent does the talking.

## What could go wrong

- **Astroturf.** If the first summits are small and staged, Washington reads them as a marketing event. The cure is patience: no national summit until there are ten real metros with real things to show.
- **Capture.** A single donor, a single company, or a single administration that becomes the network's patron. The cure is the dues model and the symmetry law.
- **The AI culture war.** Either party may decide AI skills training is the other's project. The cure is the extension frame and the libraries: nobody has found a way to make county extension agents or public libraries partisan for long.
- **Library fragility.** Federal library funding has been under pressure since 2025, and library systems are political targets in some states. The network should be a reason for libraries to exist, and should be ready to meet in community colleges and union halls where libraries can't host.
- **DC optics.** A network that looks like it exists to fly people to Washington loses the neighborhoods. The summit is one week; the district-level Labs Challenge and the members' home visits are the other fifty-one.
- **Growth before credibility.** The order of operations is metros, then report, then summit, then fellowship, then challenge. Skipping ahead produces an event with nothing to show.

## What to decide
